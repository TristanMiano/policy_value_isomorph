# Phase II results and the one-command check

**Outcome.** The stated mathematical tasks were completed under their declared assumptions. We can encode known moves and known probabilities exactly, specify the information lost by sharing board scores, and calculate the best possible imitation under a fixed expected-utility rule. The proofs distinguish representing observed behavior, learning from limited observations and explaining behavior using consequences. Checkpoint C reviewed these statements, corrected their boundaries and determined the minimum number of score levels for the archived tic-tac-toe player. **How to read the results.** The source policy is the original player's rule for choosing among legal moves. A score is a number assigned to a candidate move or to the board it produces. A direct decoder picks the highest score for the mover, with a stated priority to settle ties. A query is a question we can actually ask the player, such as which move it would choose if its first choice were unavailable. A menu is the set of moves offered in that question.

A random policy assigns probabilities to moves. In exponential sampling, a move's probability is proportional to its starting weight times the exponential of its score divided by a positive number called the temperature. Higher temperatures reduce the effect of score differences. **Support** means the moves with positive probability. A **mask** rules a move out of the draw. Probability distance here means the smallest amount of probability that must be moved between choices to turn one distribution into the other. A lottery is a list of possible consequences and their probabilities. Utility is a number assigned to each consequence, which can describe an entire play. The von Neumann–Morgenstern (VNM) rule compares moves by expected utility, the probability-weighted average utility after each move under the stated continuation.

## The temperature limits

One consequence used in the claim ledger concerns the randomness one gets by sampling from scores. **Corollary.** On a nonempty finite eligible menu, hold fixed the finite scores and strictly positive base weights. As temperature approaches zero from above, all probability concentrates on the highest-scoring eligible moves. Within that tied set, probabilities approach the base weights divided by their sum over the set. A unique best move therefore gets probability one in the limit. As temperature increases without bound, probability instead approaches the normalized base weights on the eligible menu.

**Proof.** Subtract the highest score before exponentiating. For every maximum the exponential is one at every temperature. For every strictly lower score it tends to zero at low temperature. The finite sum can be taken term by term and its limiting denominator is positive. At high temperature every finite score difference divided by temperature tends to zero, so all exponentials approach one. Previously excluded moves stay excluded. Thus in a tie a demand for one specific deterministic winner requires an explicit priority or tie convention beyond temperature alone. In the example with base weights (2,1,7) and scores (3,3,2), the low-temperature limiting probabilities are (2/3,1/3,0). [Units and finite-temperature invariance](42_score_units.md)

## Reproduce the calculations

From the repository root, run the following with Python 3.10 or newer. The `-S` option starts Python without loading installed site packages, so this check runs without a neural-network library or training data.

```sh
python -S tests/test_formal_fixtures.py
```

The command runs the examples embedded in Tasks 37–47 and independent checks using Python's standard library. In the shared-board check there are three labeled successors and six directed pairs of distinct labels. Assign no constraint, a weak comparison, or a strict comparison to each pair. Of all **729** systems, **178** admit a common score assignment and **551** do not. The test compares the constructive solver with enumeration over all 27 tables of three integer levels and checks every returned assignment. These are counts in this explicitly defined family of constraints. They describe no frequency of occurrence in real games.

For probability checks, fractions represent exact masses, including zeros and ties. The suite compares the sharp probability-shift bound in rational arithmetic, and checks logarithmic and exponential coordinates numerically at stated tolerances. It verifies both directions of play, including a player minimizing points, when generating constraints. **Self-review.** The assumption most likely to inflate an interpretation is access to the original action, probabilities or tie distribution. That access is identified at each exact reference. Conversely, a conflicting shared board or an incompatible game payoff can obscure an exact move-specific construction. Keeping both results explains when each limitation applies. The withheld-board accuracy, full-game outcomes and interpretive usefulness of independently trained value networks remain empirical questions.
