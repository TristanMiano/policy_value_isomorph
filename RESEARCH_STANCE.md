# What a value representation can tell us about a policy

A **policy** is the original rule giving a move or probabilities for available moves. A **score** is a number attached to a move or its consequences. A **decoder** uses scores to choose or sample a move. The project's **value-first** position is that relations among scores can give meaning to choices. The research aim is to recover those relations from a frozen policy, reproduce its behavior and discover what else the recovered scores predict. Reproducing the recorded move is the first comparison. Predicting alternatives, play under changed conditions and the policy's own computation call for progressively different evidence. [Original motivation](README.md)

## From truth to choice

A Boolean value is one of two possibilities, encoded by 0 and 1. If an event has probability `p`, the mean of its Boolean indicator is `p` in the unit interval. A degree of membership in a fuzzy set also lies in that interval and obtains its meaning from its membership rule. For a closed interval with distinct finite endpoints `l < u`, the increasing affine change of coordinates `(x−l)/(u−l)` maps it exactly onto [0,1], with inverse `l+(u−l)y`. Open endpoints remain open. The rescaling preserves order and averages when applied consistently. Interpreting a number as a probability further requires a probability model.

Signed real scores can express unbounded accumulated costs, differences between alternatives and gains relative to a reference. A final readout can recover discrete answers exactly. For Boolean inputs `x,y`, multiplication gives AND, `1−x` gives NOT, and returning 1 when `x+y ≥ 1` gives OR. For a finite set of moves, a score of 1 on the chosen move and 0 elsewhere recovers it when the largest legal score wins. Results for continuous inputs, noisy observations and restricted network sizes have their own error measures. Neither the Boolean examples nor the change of coordinates specifies how a neural policy is trained.

A **loss** is the quantity a training procedure minimizes. On the same feasible parameters, minimizing a loss `L` has exactly the same solutions as maximizing `−L`. Its derivative reverses sign wherever it exists. This assigns meaning relative to the training objective. A player's payoff and a hidden unit's activity each require their own interpretation. The possibility that postponing discrete readouts improves learning or interpretability is a research hypothesis about architectures, training conditions and tasks.

## Four meanings a score can acquire

| Meaning | Evidence and question it licenses |
| --- | --- |
| Decoder value | A specified score and decoding rule reproduce moves or probabilities. An indicator can encode a deterministic move. Log probabilities can encode a random choice, with zero probability represented by exclusion or negative infinity. How much fidelity survives restricted data or a smaller model? |
| Revealed value | Choices when available moves or outcome probabilities change constrain a preference ordering or numerical ratios. A single selected move reveals its priority over some alternatives under an assumed rule. Comparing two unchosen alternatives requires other observations or assumptions. |
| Return value | A return is the payoff accumulated over a play under a declared environment, horizon, perspective and continuation rule. Its expectation is a move score. The original player can choose a different move from the one with the best return. |
| Mechanistic value | An internal quantity participates causally in making the choice. Predicting an external score from activity suggests where to look. An intervention that changes the activity and measures the ensuing choices, with matched controls, addresses causal involvement. |

**Existence** asks for a construction on a specified domain. **Usefulness** asks how accurately or economically the construction answers further questions. **Identifiability** asks which distinctions the available observations can determine. **Mechanism** concerns how this particular player computes a choice. Different scores can agree on every observed move. For example, scores (2,1) and (100,0) prefer the same first move but give different sampling probabilities if exponentiated at the same scale. Nonuniqueness restricts what can be inferred about utility and suggests new queries. It leaves the displayed behavioral construction intact.

A **latent reward** is a proposed payoff rule inferred from observations of the source. Predicting new choices supplies evidence for that explanation. A claim that the original network actually uses it calls for controlled changes to the network and measurement of its subsequent choices. The four meanings above can be investigated separately. ## The two comparisons in the games

First train and freeze a source player. Estimate the fraction of its visits to each board on which it takes each legal move, keeping the counts and denominators. A board network scores the resulting board and a board-and-move network scores the candidate directly. Each can choose from its fitted scores. For **von Neumann–Morgenstern (VNM) expected-utility selection**, assign one utility to each complete play, simulate the consequences of forcing each candidate first move, then compare the probability-weighted means of that same utility. A learned utility can use the final fitted move score or a time-weighted sum of fitted scores. Declare who continues each seat, the perspective and any access to the frozen player. A supplied win/draw/loss payoff gives another comparison. Record both copying the original choices and winning complete games. **Self-review.** One reading allows a score to memorize every source action. Another asks a single compact utility to explain new choices under changed continuations. The first gives a precise existence question and the second retains the explanatory aim. We carry both, along with the supplied-payoff comparison. A failure of one compact utility on one board constrains that utility and its continuation assumption.
