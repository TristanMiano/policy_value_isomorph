# From a player's moves to scores in three board games

**Phase V research report · 22 November 2026 · Tasks 73–82**

How closely can scores learned from watching a player reproduce its choices? And how often does the reconstructed player win? A **source** is the original neural player, frozen before we collect its moves. A **V network** sees the board resulting from a proposed move. A **Q network** also sees the preceding board and which move was proposed. Both learn the observed fraction of visits taking each move. **Direct selection** takes the largest immediate fitted frequency. **Von Neumann–Morgenstern (VNM) selection** tries each legal move and chooses the one with the highest expected numerical score of a completed game, from the current player's perspective.

**The definition of the complete-game score changed the answer.** In the planned comparison, only the frequency of the final move counted. Using this score with Q lowered move agreement in every independent original across all three games. Its win rate against the study opponents also fell on average in each game. Giving earlier moves weight changed both results. In Connect Four, Q won **57.45 ± 11.30%** of games choosing directly, **49.37 ± 17.69%** scoring the final move, and **63.46 ± 16.60%** scoring all moves with progressively less weight. All three rules used exactly the same fitted frequencies. The ± values are sample standard deviations, the spread across 24 independently trained originals, each evaluated with two board partitions and two fresh fits per partition. [Absolute results, paired differences and uncertainty](experiments/phase_v/tables.md)

The follow-up traced the two drops in agreement on the original boards. Fitting every opening and continuation score exactly isolated a change due to the final-move utility. Scoring every turn with a discount of 0.9 preserved every choice in the fully covered fixed-player control. Additional training coverage and fitting move probabilities jointly improved agreement on withheld boards in the larger games. [Diagnosis, corrections and controls](AGREEMENT_DIAGNOSIS.md)

The later win-rate study compared 56 expected-utility formulations and froze a new standard before evaluating it on separate originals. Checkpoint F replayed the original and both reconstructed players against matched opponents and tested what each reconstruction chooses when the original's favorite disappears. The Phase V tables below document the earlier final-move comparison. [Checkpoint F review and release decision](CHECKPOINT_F.md) · [Prospective VNM standard and self-play results](experiments/win_selection/README.md)

## What the players learned

- **Tic-tac-toe.** Each of 32 sources learned to choose a move preserving the best result available against perfect replies. Each had an independently drawn preference order among equally good cells. Two layers of 128 units that retain positive inputs and set negative inputs to zero took the board and side to move as input. Training minimized the negative log probability of the prescribed move. Each source matched its own prescribed move on all 4,520 reachable decision boards and gave up zero guaranteed game points. Each recovery fit learned from observed moves, with all 4,520 boards visited in collection and 729–1,111 boards reserved per partition. [Source, fitting settings and counts](PHASE_IV.md#how-the-players-were-trained-and-what-the-scores-could-represent)
- **Connect Four.** Twelve sources learned a teacher that scores a column with two units for winning immediately and one for occupying a column where the opponent could win immediately, taking the highest sum. Another twelve learned just a personal column ordering. Ties used each teacher’s independently shuffled order. Actual frozen network moves define the observations. Each source saw 4,416–4,532 distinct positions drawn from random legal games. We optimized a shared pattern network to increase the probability of the teacher move on 3,873–3,998 fitting boards, checking progress on the remaining boards. The Q inputs include explicit immediate-win and opponent-threat features, which are especially informative for these teachers. Against the common opponents the first group won **68.75 ± 6.78%** and the second **48.44 ± 5.55%** (12 originals each). These sources have measured playing strength and distinct habits. Connect Four optimality was outside the measurement. [Source training](src/policy_value_isomorph/phase_v.py) · [Original outcomes by seat and teacher](experiments/phase_v/sources.json)
- **4×4 Reversi.** A placement brackets and flips opponent discs. When only the opponent can place a disc, the player must pass. The game ends when neither can place a disc, and the player with more discs wins. The teacher takes the first legal move in row order preserving the best guaranteed final outcome. From the standard opening that outcome for Black is a loss. Each of 24 networks first learned shared board patterns from a sample of 6,500 decision boards, then learned an extra output correction for each remaining disagreement across the entire game. Shared training alone matched **52,036–52,280 of 56,621** decision boards. The corrected originals all matched **56,621/56,621**, giving up zero guaranteed points. These original networks share the same favorite at every board. Independently initialized weights can distinguish their lower-ranked moves. The reconstructions had no access to the correction when trained on visits. ## Positions, opponents and uncertainty

A **holdout** is a group of positions kept out of fitting. We independently drew two board partitions and two game collections for each original. Within a partition, each network had two fresh initializations. Related rotations or reflections were kept together. V training also excluded resulting-board inputs shared with reserved examples. A separate validation group selected settings. The two new games therefore contribute 96 paired fits each per source behavior and network type. For tic-tac-toe we recovered the prespecified comparisons from the 128 paired Phase IV fits per type and behavior. For each game we first averaged the four fits belonging to an original. Counting every move or fit as an independent original would understate uncertainty. [Design simulation](experiments/phase_v/planning.json) · [Saved block-level differences](experiments/phase_v/primary.json)

For each new-game collection, two copies of the frozen player finished games from the ordinary opening and 384 randomly sampled legal starting positions. A fixed-choice original played 480 games. A randomized version, which takes the favorite with probability ¾ and otherwise samples uniformly, played 1,248. Connect Four averaged **4,237 ± 793** observed decision boards for fixed sources and **13,464 ± 2,757** for randomized sources. Reversi averaged **1,187 ± 26** and **2,600 ± 72** respectively (24 original blocks per game). On a separate pool of random legal prefixes we sampled 160 distinct withheld Connect Four boards per partition. For Reversi we sampled 128 positions with multiple placements, 32 with one placement and 24 with a forced pass per partition. Their weights restore each group's share of the withheld reachable population. The data also give separate estimates weighted by the frequency of a board in games played by the original. [Raw paths, counts and weights](experiments/phase_v/README.md)

For playing strength we gave equal weight to playing first and second and to four opponent groups. In the new games these were uniform legal moves, the favorite legal moves of two independently initialized untrained networks, a positional rule and a tactical rule. The positional rule prefers central Connect Four columns, or Reversi corners then edges. The tactical player uses the immediate-win and blocking scores above followed by centrality in Connect Four, or the optimal row-order teacher in Reversi. Lowest column or cell resolves remaining ties. Per fit we used 32 opening–opponent pairs, each played in both seats. Half the openings were the ordinary start, half random prefixes up to six turns. This gives **6,144 played games per new game per fixed rule**. Tic-tac-toe used exact probabilities of full games from the empty board against uniform play, five untrained networks, a win/block/center/corner player and a lowest-cell optimal player, with equal weight for the four groups. [Original outcomes](experiments/phase_v/sources.json) · [Tic-tac-toe design](PHASE_IV_PROTOCOL.md)

**Agreement** is the probability of the same move on the specified withheld positions. **Win rate** is the probability of winning against the opponent group just defined. **Game points** average +1 for a win, 0 for a draw and −1 for a loss. A **paired difference** subtracts direct selection using the same fitted weights, positions, opponents and openings. A 95% confidence interval estimates uncertainty in its mean. A two-sided p-value measures how extreme the observed difference would be under a zero-mean reference model. Holm's adjustment accounts for examining the 24 planned differences together. The tables give individual intervals from independent originals and from 5,000 resamplings of entire originals. They include the spread, raw and adjusted p-values, and counts of originals gaining, tying or losing. Variation includes source training, collections, partitions, fitting and the sampled opponents and games. Each named game receives one third of the cross-game weight. The three games themselves were selected by design. [Methods, raw and adjusted p-values](experiments/phase_v/tables.md) · [Statistical implementation](scripts/report_phase_v.py)

## Agreement and games won

For the primary VNM rule, play each candidate to the end with both seats taking their own highest immediate score from the fitted network. Use the final move's fitted frequency as the whole-game utility, positive for a first-player move and negative for a second-player move. The first player maximizes that score and the second minimizes it. The lowest legal move number breaks ties. A fixed continuation needs one path per candidate. A separate comparison uses original continuations, sampling four paths per candidate in the new games and sixteen in tic-tac-toe when the original is randomized. The alternatives score every signed move in the path, either at full weight or with successive weights 1, 0.9, 0.81 and so on. A pass takes one turn. These utilities come from the same visit-trained network. [Protocol](PHASE_V_PROTOCOL.md) · [Path scoring and move selection](src/policy_value_isomorph/phase_v_native.cpp)

## How close can the representation get?

On a finite board collection one can assign each resulting board a numerical level, choose the move leading to the highest level and use the original tie convention. We constructed such scores and checked every choice. On tic-tac-toe all 32 originals admit an exact construction on 4,520 decisions. On Reversi, **three levels reproduce all 56,621 decisions** for the row-order optimal original. However, the same resulting board sometimes comes from both a selected and an unselected move. Exactly **4,124 of 62,788** Reversi successor boards have those conflicting labels. The least possible mean squared frequency error for a shared resulting-board score on all **89,332** legal candidates is **0.02981**. The construction and the error bound measure different properties of the same population. [Tic-tac-toe constructions](experiments/phase_iv/finite_bounds.jsonl.gz) · [Reversi and Connect Four constructions](experiments/phase_v/bounds.json)

For 23 of 24 Connect Four originals the constructed ordering matched every choice in the first fixed collection's tested board set. For the remaining original the construction matched **3,657/3,659** and incompatible order requirements bound the maximum at **3,658/3,659**. The tested sets contained 3,206–5,739 boards per original. Extrapolation to the entire game would require more positions or a proof. A separate rules check compared 3,481 Connect Four candidate transitions with the repository's independent game implementation and checked all 89,332 Reversi transitions with a second implementation of disc flipping, passing and the optimal-game recurrence. ## Fitting error and individual mistakes

For each observed parent board we divide every move count by all visits to that board, including zero counts for legal alternatives. V and Q optimize half the sum of squared differences from these fractions, with one equal contribution per eligible legal candidate. The reported mean squared error is twice that objective divided by the number of candidates, in squared-frequency units. The readout sums twelve active board-pattern weights, initialized independently where observed. A pattern is a fixed detector for a cell configuration, turn, disc count or tactical feature. Training updates the readout by the gradient divided by how often its pattern occurs, at step size 0.75/12. Validation selects among 4, 16 and 48 full passes. Raw outputs are bounded to [0,1] for action scoring. Scores for separate legal moves can sum to more or less than one. [Fitting objective and code](src/policy_value_isomorph/phase_v.py) · [Pattern definitions](src/policy_value_isomorph/phase_v_rules.hpp)

## Copying, winning and explaining changed moves

The **move imitator** learns by maximizing the probability of observed moves. In the new games it has the same board-and-move pattern features and adjustable-weight count as Q. The **revealed order** fits the first three favorites elicited by successively removing chosen moves on up to 192 fitting boards and 64 validation boards. The **cell-frequency rule** pools observed move counts by action. **Distillation** fits numerical outputs from the original, which requires additional information in training. An additional **supplied-payoff VNM** rule plays out Q direct copies and scores a completed game by its actual win, draw or loss. A **fitted-payoff VNM** variant learns a common value for a draw from observed choices. Separate game-outcome networks predict game points or frequencies of wins, draws and losses from observed endings. These controls give different information to the learner. A **temperature** divides a score before exponentiation, giving each move sampling weight exp(score/temperature). Higher temperature spreads probability more widely. **Probability error** is the fraction of move probability that must be reassigned to reproduce the original distribution. [Methods and access](experiments/phase_v/README.md)

## Interpretation

A score can encode an entire finite player's choices even when a network trained on a small sample has difficulty predicting them. The Reversi construction makes that distinction exact. The empirical comparisons then separate three objectives. The original's immediate choice is often predictable directly from its move frequencies. The move that wins against a specified opponent depends on how that opponent replies. Predicting the original's second choice after its first is removed requires information about alternatives. The original games reveal those alternatives unevenly. Reporting all three outcomes identifies which interpretation a result supports. The uncertainty intervals describe the stated players and panels in these three games. Different teachers, features and board distributions across games limit further generalization.
