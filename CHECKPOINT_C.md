# Checkpoint C · Which scores can reproduce a player's choices?

**Decision, 2026-11-20.** A **policy** is a player's rule for choosing a move or assigning probabilities to moves. A **decoder** is the rule that reads numbers called scores and uses them to choose. Phase II establishes exact ways to encode known behavior and describes which information the decoder preserves. The review also found how few different board scores the archived tic-tac-toe player needs. Learning those scores from incomplete visits, winning games with a fitted model, and explaining what the original network uses internally remain separate research questions. [Phase II results and proofs](formalism/48_finite_checks.md) · [Claim ledger](CLAIM_LEDGER.md)

## Three score levels reproduce all 4,520 tic-tac-toe choices

The **source** here is the frozen neural player trained to choose the lowest-numbered cell among moves that give the best outcome it can guarantee against perfect replies. It matched that rule on every reachable decision board. Cells are numbered 0 through 8 across the rows. We read its archived move counts from games started once at each decision board. On each board exactly one move had positive count. We gave each of the 4,520 boards equal weight and considered all 16,167 legal board–move pairs. We assigned one score to each of the 5,477 distinct resulting boards. Both players select the largest score because it represents the original mover's choice. [Source training and verification](NEURAL_TICTACTOE_CONTROL.md) · [Archived counts](experiments/tictactoe_sampling/counts.npz)

| Requirement on the chosen move | Minimum distinct score levels | Original choices reproduced | Boards with a tie at the highest constructed score |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Score strictly above every other move | 7 | 4,520/4,520 | 0/4,520 |
| Highest score, lowest-numbered cell wins ties | 3 | 4,520/4,520 | 2,143/4,520 |

**Why three is the minimum.** Compare each chosen move with each alternative from the same board. Of the 11,647 comparisons, 3,275 require a strictly higher score because the rival would win a tie. The other 8,372 allow equal scores. In the archive, two strict comparisons form a chain of three resulting boards. The boards, written row by row with a dot for an empty cell, are `O.XO.X...` below `..XO.X..O` below `..X..XO.O`. Every assignment honoring the source's choices therefore needs at least three different scores.

To attain that lower bound, start boards with no predecessors at zero. In an order that visits every lower board first, assign each board the greatest number of strict increases on any chain ending there. Every required comparison then holds. The largest number of increases is two with the source's tie rule and six when every comparison must be strict. Reading the scores back reproduced every archived choice in both cases. This independent recount also found that 2,026/5,477 resulting boards were selected from one parent and rejected from another. Those conflicting frequency labels still permit exact choices. The four-level reduction describes distinct numbers used in a lookup that had access to every board. It gives no estimate of accuracy on a withheld board or of the smallest neural network. ## Corrections made in the review

- **A chosen example of a score table does not identify the original numbers.** Scores (0,1) and (1,2) both choose the second move. Encoding that move as one point for the winner gives back (0,1) in both cases. In mathematics an *isomorphism of sets* means a one-to-one correspondence with a map that works backwards in both directions. For this example it holds between moves and the tables specifically defined to contain one 1 and otherwise zeros. More generally, a *response class* is the set of all scores giving the same answers to the declared questions. These classes are in one-to-one correspondence with the answer patterns that actually occur. Each pattern maps back to its entire class. Picking one member gives a reproducible convention. [Corrected inverse and proof](formalism/41_response_equivalence.md)
- **The route to a board can change its expected payoff.** Here payoff is the number of points earned at the end of play. In a constructed game, two routes reach the same board and the player who finishes the game remembers the route. Its rule earns +1 after the first route and −1 after the second. Any single board score has an error of at least 1 point on one route, and zero attains that smallest worst error. Retaining the route recovers both payoffs exactly. A shared board return is valid when all the histories it combines have the same average future payoff under the chosen rules. [Corrected board-return condition](formalism/40_successor_scores.md)
- **Random choices require specifying what was observed.** Imagine one player tossing a fresh fair coin on each query and another tossing once and repeating the answer. Each answers A with probability ½ on each query. The probability of two consecutive answers agreeing is ½ for the first and 1 for the second. Comparing single answers and comparing sequences therefore preserve different information. When inferring shared scores across different sets of legal moves, repeated observations of the same pair must also imply compatible odds. A move with zero observations may still have positive probability. [Observation rule](formalism/41_response_equivalence.md) · [Conditions for learning score differences](formalism/44_probability_ratios.md)
- **Several reasons for random play can produce the same behavior.** A *utility* gives a number to each complete play. The **von Neumann–Morgenstern (VNM) rule** compares moves by the average of that utility after the move, under stated rules for continuing the game. In a one-move game, splitting a tie equally, tossing a fair coin to decide which of two opposite utilities to maximize, and sampling two equal scores with equal starting weights all give each move probability ½. Their probability difference is zero on that observation. For a fixed utility, the smallest probability mass a rule confined to its best moves must reassign still equals the source's probability on lower-utility moves, provided it can freely choose how to split each tie. [Corrected comparison and proof](formalism/45_expected_utility.md)

| [37 · Fixed choices](formalism/37_finite_choices.md) | One point for the chosen move copies any known deterministic policy on nonempty finite menus. Each winner must strictly beat rivals ahead of it in tie priority and may tie later ones. A terminal position has no decision. |
| [38 · Discrete probabilities](formalism/38_random_choices.md) | Known probabilities determine scores on positive-probability moves up to an added constant for each menu, with fixed positive starting weights and temperature (the scale controlling the influence of score differences). Eligible moves with zero probability need exclusion or negative infinity. On infinitely many moves the total sampling weight must be positive and finite. |
| [39 · Continuous moves](formalism/39_metric_actions.md) | Scoring by closeness uniquely selects a known feasible target when only identical actions are at zero distance. On an open set the closest move may never be attained. The distance of an approximate choice is at most the smallest approachable distance plus twice the largest score error plus the search tolerance. |
| [40 · Scores shared across boards](formalism/40_successor_scores.md) | For finitely many deterministic moves and resulting boards, all specified choices can share one score per board exactly when the required comparisons contain no directed circle with a strict increase. The new archive recount checks weaker ties and the minimum levels. An action and its history can always carry a move-specific score. |
| [41 · Observable score classes](formalism/41_response_equivalence.md) | Freeze the two maps between response classes and attainable answer patterns described above. For fixed starting weights and temperature, a discrete probability distribution also has a unique representative if scores are normalized so their exponential weights sum to one on eligible moves. |
| [42 · Score units](formalism/42_score_units.md) | Strictly increasing changes of units preserve ranks. Rescaling score gaps and temperature together preserves sampling probabilities. A common positive rescaling and shift preserve average-utility choices among lotteries (probabilities over consequences). Where all mixtures and sure values in an interval can be compared, requiring their ordering and indifferences to survive forces that form. |
| [43 · Ranking alternatives](formalism/43_menu_queries.md) | With one fixed strict order on n moves, removing each freshly chosen winner reveals the order in n−1 queries, including zero queries for a singleton. A player can give consistent answers to every pair yet choose differently on a larger menu. |
| [44 · Recovering score gaps](formalism/44_probability_ratios.md) | For a rule that samples in proportion to starting weight times the exponential of score divided by temperature, exact odds give the score gap in temperature units after adjusting for starting weights. Comparisons must connect the moves, agree across repeated pairs and preserve which moves can occur. Finite counts give estimates with sampling uncertainty. |
| [45 · VNM complete-play utility](formalism/45_expected_utility.md) | A single utility of the allowed consequences must satisfy all observed move comparisons for the given environment, both continuation players, perspective and tie rule. The best attainable probability match allows a separate split of each tie. A predetermined split can reassign additional mass. |
| [46 · Score errors](formalism/46_stability.md) | Subtract the true score from each estimated score. If the winning margin exceeds the difference between the largest and smallest of these errors, the winner stays the same. At equality a rival can tie and win by priority. The probability-change bound is attained by a two-move example at fixed temperature and starting weights. Agreement with the original player also depends on how the reference scores select moves. |
| [47 · Unobserved distinctions](formalism/47_ambiguities.md) | The six examples preserve the declared observations while varying gaps, score units, unoffered moves, intermediate rewards, hidden histories or unvisited decisions. Repeated observations can distinguish some constructions when they expose information absent from a single draw. |
| [48 · Executable checks](formalism/48_finite_checks.md) | The finite examples cover ties, exclusions, both directions of play and approximate scores. Of 729 ways to specify absent, weak or strict comparisons on the six ordered pairs of three distinct labels, direct enumeration and the score construction find the same 178 feasible systems. These counts describe that constructed family. |

**Self-review.** Allowing source-specific tie rules can hide information in the rule for reading scores. Here the lowest-cell priority was fixed independently by the archived study. Requiring a unique winner as a second comparison shows exactly how much the priority contributes. Access to every source move also makes fitting easier. The empirical obligations remain to measure agreement and game outcomes after fitting from limited visits, and to test whether learned scores predict fresh choices when the options change.
