# Checkpoint E · What can scores recover from a tic-tac-toe player?

For every one of 32 independently trained optimal players, we could assign one score to each resulting board and reproduce **all 4,520 choices** by taking the highest-scoring board. Learning scores from part of the game was harder. The best rule for reproducing choices depended on whether we gave every unfamiliar board a vote or concentrated on the boards the original player actually visited. The best rule for winning games differed again. [Original research question](README.md) · [Phase IV study](PHASE_IV.md) · [Finite constructions](experiments/phase_iv/finite_bounds.jsonl.gz)

## Which boards count?

The **original player** learned a move that guarantees the best available result against perfect replies, with a separately drawn priority order for equally good cells. All 32 networks copied their assigned move on 4,520/4,520 reachable decision boards, giving up zero guaranteed payoff. We then froze their weights. The **randomized player** takes its frozen network's move with probability ¾ and otherwise draws uniformly among legal cells. The **move imitator** is a separately trained network predicting the observed moves or their probabilities directly.

The **board network (V)** scores the board after a candidate move. The **board-and-move network (Q)** scores the current board and candidate together. Both learn the observed count for a move divided by visits to its parent. **Direct** takes the highest fitted frequency. **Von Neumann–Morgenstern (VNM) selection** tries each legal move, plays to the end, and chooses the best expected whole-game score for the mover. Here **discounted** means adding fitted move frequencies with weights 1, 0.9, 0.81 and so on. Each X move contributes positively and each O move negatively. Both seats continue with the same fitted direct rule. One continuation per candidate gives the exact expectation under these fixed rules. Ties go to the lowest-numbered legal cell. These reconstructions can play without asking the original for moves. [Architecture, counts, losses and example disagreements](PHASE_IV.md#how-the-players-were-trained-and-what-the-scores-could-represent)

Boards were withheld from reconstruction training. **Equal-board** results average all 729–1,111 withheld decision boards per partition. **Self-play** results weight those same boards by their probability of being reached from the empty opening when the original plays both seats, then divide by the sum of those weights. Only **1–6** withheld boards had positive weight for each fixed original. All withheld boards had positive weight for each randomized original. The opening was always withheld. Thus self-play agreement estimates what happens along a narrow path for the fixed original.

In the tables, ± is one sample standard deviation across 32 original players. Each original contributes the average of two independently randomized board partitions with two new fitting initializations per partition. **Wins** come from complete games in both seats against four equally weighted opponent groups. These draw uniformly, choose using five independently initialized untrained networks (equal weight within the group), take an immediate win then block then center then corner then any cell, or take the best guaranteed outcome. Neural opponents take their highest-scoring legal move. Fixed opponents resolve ties by lowest cell. **Game points** award +1 for a win, 0 for a draw and −1 for a loss. The board weights affect the copying measure. Games always start at the empty board.

For randomized players, **probability error** is the fraction of move probability that must be reassigned to match the original distribution, averaged over boards. Smaller is closer. Even two independent draws from the same distribution can differ. **Temperature** controls how broadly a player samples moves. Each legal move receives weight proportional to the exponential of its score divided by temperature. The fitted copies still choose directly when finishing a hypothetical game. The sampling rule applies when the evaluated player chooses its actual move.

## Which results depend on the kind of player?

- **Restricted random choices.** A player assigned ¾ to its first-ranked move and ¼ to its second, taking the sole legal move on forced boards. Reading its full probabilities gives exact recovery under either weighting. Estimating them from 64 independent choices per withheld board gave probability errors of **4.09 ± 0.08%** with equal boards and **4.41 ± 1.58%** with self-play weights. Between 10 and 68 withheld boards carried positive self-play weight per partition. The corresponding errors with one choice were 35.59 ± 0.64% and 38.28 ± 7.77%. This probe measured probabilities at queried boards. A whole-game strength estimate for that count-only reconstruction would require specifying its rule elsewhere. [Paired changes and uncertainty](experiments/checkpoint_e.json)
- **Choices that change with the question.** On 2,048 sampled board cases, asking for the best remaining move repeatedly recovered the stable underlying order for all 12,128 candidate pairs. When the player reversed its order on offers of exactly two moves, the same procedure agreed with the unedited order on 10,080 pairs. Here the unit is a queried pair. In the separate experiment offering a sure draw against a win/loss gamble, a shared fitted draw value missed 6.68 ± 1.22% of changed-odds choices for a player valuing a draw at 0.375 on a loss-to-win scale of −1 to +1. Transferring that fitted rule to a player choosing the first displayed option gave 50.37 ± 3.20% error. These questions have no board-visitation distribution or game opponent. The board player itself was only asked questions its interface could receive. [Query budgets, comparisons and observed answers](PHASE_IV.md#coverage-uncertain-consequences-and-changed-choices)
- **Earlier pilot players.** The pilot enumerated all 4,520 boards for each of five specified players. Its VNM rule valued only final game points and assumed the original finished both seats. The lowest-cell perfect player picked the earliest move preserving its best guaranteed result. The priority player used win/block/center/corner/any. The missed-win player passed up an immediate win for the lowest available nonwinning move whenever possible, following priority otherwise. The pilot neural player learned priority on 528 boards and took its highest-scoring legal cell. The randomized priority player mixed priority with a uniform draw in the same ¾-to-¼ ratio. The figures below are exact for those five players and that fixed opponent panel. The pilot supplied one instance of each rule. ## What accounts for the remaining disagreement?

**A limitation of the target.** Across the 32 fixed originals, **2,052 ± 38 of 5,477** resulting boards had different target frequencies depending on the preceding board. A single score for each resulting board has a minimum mean squared frequency error of **0.08667 ± 0.00152** across all 16,167 legal board–move pairs. Nevertheless an ordering with 7–9 distinct score levels reproduced every choice of each player. This is a construction using full information. Frequency prediction and move ordering place different demands on a score. For a randomized player, always choosing the most probable move still has probability error equal to one minus that probability on each board.

**A limitation that shrank with training.** Keeping the data, penalty and 192-unit Q architecture fixed, fitting its output weights to convergence increased equal-board agreement from **68.20 ± 3.76%** after one update to **78.50 ± 2.17%**. In another comparison with 2,048 observed source moves, opening-only fixed self-play visited nine boards. Choosing relatively unvisited legal starting positions covered **871 ± 20**. More training and broader sampling address different sources of error. The primary fits trained only the output of a randomly initialized hidden layer, whereas the imitator trained all its layers. The measured gap to exact copying leaves open what a fully trained value network with matched capacity and observation budgets could recover. [Training errors and counts on two actual disagreement boards](PHASE_IV.md#how-the-players-were-trained-and-what-the-scores-could-represent) · [Training and coverage comparisons](experiments/phase_iv/diagnostics.jsonl.gz)

**What a recovered score explains.** Complete choices can determine an ordering while leaving many numerical utilities compatible with it. The best observed independently deployable predictor when a move disappeared was the move imitator, with **12.71 ± 1.38%** error for fixed originals, compared with **21.16 ± 1.65%** for Q direct. Reading the original's scores after hiding the removed move reproduced its exact answer under that menu rule, using extra access. Predicting a changed choice tests one observable meaning of preference. Identifying a calculation inside the original network would require interventions on that network. ## Methods to carry into Connect Four and 4×4 Reversi

| Purpose in both games | Methods to retain and reason |
| --- | --- |
| Copy the original | Use the fully trained move imitator as the behavioral benchmark. Carry **Q direct** for ordinary self-play positions and **Q discounted VNM with fitted copies** for broader coverage. Report both board weightings. For randomized sources, sample both scores on the same temperature grid and include a setting selected using validation counts. Carry 0.05 and 0.25 as specified candidates on the normalized frequency scale. The preferred temperature changed with the board weights. |
| Play the game well | Lead with **V discounted VNM with fitted copies**, paired with **V direct**. Score actual wins, draws, losses and points against the frozen opponents from matching openings in both seats. Also play each VNM rule head-to-head against its own direct rule. This addresses how accurately the assumed replies describe the real opponent. |
| Predict what the player values | Freeze predictions for the favorite's removal and for removal of a different move. Compare the **move imitator**, **Q direct**, a ranking learned by asking for the best remaining move, and a **compact VNM utility** that fixes win and loss and fits one common draw value. Include the supplied win/draw/loss utility and the original's raw move scores as access-labeled references. Score new answers and count source queries. Treat claims about internal computation separately. |

Keep the entire Task 81b comparison on the **same V and Q fits** in each game. Compare direct with VNM using (1) the signed fitted frequency of the final move, (2) the signed sum over all moves, and (3) the sum with factor 0.9 per turn. Run each with both seats continued by fitted direct copies and again by original-player copies, recording the extra original calls. Retain final-move VNM with fitted copies as the primary comparison across games, including the tic-tac-toe rows where agreement fell. The supplied final payoff and learned draw value are separate utility references. For randomized move rules compare the full probabilities as well as agreement between independent draws.

**Connect Four** needs both ordinary openings and varied legal positions so that rare columns and replies receive observations. Use the declared mix of original strengths, with the winning and copying comparisons paired by opponent, opening and seat. **4×4 Reversi** can use its reachable positions and best guaranteed outcomes as references. Count positions with multiple placements, a single placement and a required pass separately. A pass changes the side to move. On multi-placement boards, remove a favorite and observe the next placement. Both games need their own advance repetition and simulation budgets. Tic-tac-toe's effect sizes describe these players and this opponent panel. [Game-specific tasks and the common comparison](TODO.md#phase-v--interpretive-leverage-in-larger-neural-game-policies)

**Review check.** The replay reads saved choices, scores, board partitions and both-seat outcomes. It verifies the original's tie-broken optimal move and the resulting-board score construction on every decision for all 32 players. It reconstructs the two copying measures from legal move distributions, sums the declared opponent weights, and checks each reviewed deployment against its saved measurements before computing the paired results. The initial design fixed the primary comparison before training. This review revisited promising methods afterward, which is why selection itself still needs testing in each new game. A single self-play path could favor an opening specialist, while an equal-board analysis could conceal poor play from the opening. Retaining both populations and the methods that lost under each gives the next games a way to distinguish those explanations.
