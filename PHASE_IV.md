# Phase IV · When does looking ahead copy a player, and when does it beat one?

We trained **32 different optimal tic-tac-toe players**. Each learned to preserve the best result it could guarantee against perfect replies, using a randomly chosen cell order to break ties. We froze each player before collecting any of its moves. A reconstruction learned from counts of those moves. We asked how often it copied the original on unfamiliar boards, how often it won against other players, and what it predicted when a preferred move disappeared.

The answer depended on which moves in a hypothetical game contributed to its score. Using only the **final move** made both networks copy the original less often. Counting moves throughout the game, with later moves receiving less weight, improved copying for both. And a reconstruction could beat its own direct player head-to-head while losing more often against other opponents. ## The main comparison

The **board network (V)** scores the board resulting from a move. The **board-and-move network (Q)** scores the current board and candidate move together. **Direct selection** takes the highest fitted move frequency. **Von Neumann–Morgenstern (VNM) selection** tries a candidate move, finishes the game by a declared rule, and chooses the move with the highest average whole-game score, or *utility*, for the mover. In the primary comparison both sides finish by taking their fitted network's direct move. The final move's fitted frequency is the whole-game utility, positive for an X move and negative for an O move. X maximizes and O minimizes. Ties go to the lowest-numbered legal cell. These players choose deterministically, so one complete continuation per candidate gives the exact expectation. They use no original-player queries during selection.

**Agreement** gives every withheld decision board one vote. **Wins, draws and losses** are probabilities from the empty opening, with half weight for each seat. Four equally weighted opponent groups choose in different ways. One draws uniformly among legal moves. Five newly initialized, untrained networks each take their highest-scoring legal move and share one group's weight equally. A priority player takes a win, blocks a win, takes the center, takes a corner, or takes any move, in that order. A perfect-reply player chooses the best guaranteed result. The last three groups resolve ties by lowest cell number. Each reconstructed player uses its own selection rule on every turn. All possible game outcomes were enumerated.

Each cell in the table gives **mean ± sample standard deviation**, in percent. Each of the 32 independent units consists of one original player, a fresh opponent panel, two randomized partitions of the boards, and two independently initialized fits per partition. We averaged the four fits inside each unit first. The standard deviation describes variation among these 32 unit averages. The original player's agreement is an exact comparison against itself.

A **paired change** subtracts direct selection from VNM on the same network, boards, seats and opponents. A win earns +1 point, a draw 0, and a loss −1, so **mean points per game** equal win probability minus loss probability. The **95% confidence intervals** estimate average paired changes over repetitions of this design. The **p-value** is the probability of a test statistic at least this extreme in either direction under a model with zero mean paired change. We used paired Student intervals and tests on the 32 unit averages, and Holm's adjustment across the six planned comparisons. The intervals apply individually. The results file also gives intervals from resampling whole units 10,000 times. **Who was on the other side mattered.** We also played the VNM method directly against the direct rule from the *same fit*, swapping seats. Across 128 fits this gives 256 deterministic games per network type. V VNM won 198, drew 44 and lost 14. Q VNM won 191, drew 48 and lost 17. Across the 32 independent units, VNM's win rates were **77.34 ± 16.93%** for V and **74.61 ± 21.41%** for Q. In this matchup the direct opponent makes exactly the replies assumed during planning. For Q, the comparison against the external panel went in the opposite direction. On withheld boards, direct alone matched the original on 19.95 ± 2.11% of positions for Q, while VNM alone matched on 8.66 ± 1.06%. ## Which rule would we choose for each aim?

These rankings describe this experiment. Each fit's settings were chosen on its validation boards. The rankings across methods use their measured performance on common evaluation cases. Every entry, including ties and uncertainty in differences from the leader, is retained. Methods using full original-player information have their own access labels. - **To copy the original player's fixed moves**, the best observed visit-trained V/Q rule was Q VNM with a **discounted sum** and fitted copies. This counts each fitted move frequency positively for X and negatively for O, with weights 1, 0.9, 0.81 and so on starting at the candidate move. It matched **81.06 ± 1.90%**, a gain of **2.67 percentage points** [2.23, 3.11] over Q direct. With V, the same utility raised agreement by **2.52 points** [1.73, 3.32], to **76.48 ± 2.03%**. A separate *move imitator*, a fully trained network with one output for each cell, matched **82.59 ± 2.14%**. Its advantage over that Q rule was **1.53 points** [0.80, 2.26].
- **To earn game points with visit-trained networks acting independently of the source**, V VNM with the same discounted sum and fitted copies led at **0.322 ± 0.086 points per game** for fixed players. Its difference from V direct was **+0.172** [0.141, 0.203]. It won **46.11 ± 4.28%**, drew **39.96 ± 4.52%** and lost **13.93 ± 5.40%**. Giving the final move all the weight had produced a much smaller gain. The original players themselves earned **0.455 ± 0.060 points**. Payoff-based references with access to the original continuations are included in the full table.
- **To predict a player's next choice when a move disappears**, the move imitator had the lowest error among the learned, independently deployable explanations for fixed players. We gave equal weight to removing the original favorite and removing a different move. It missed **12.71 ± 1.38%**, compared with **21.16 ± 1.65%** for Q direct, an advantage of **8.45 percentage points** [8.00, 8.89]. Reading the original network's scores and taking their highest remaining entry reproduces its answer exactly under the declared menu rule. This comparison measures prediction after a change in what is offered. It gives no observation of the network's internal reasons for choosing a move.
- **For a randomized player**, we measured the share of probability that must be moved between legal actions to reproduce its distribution. Smaller is closer. This player follows the frozen network with probability ¾ and otherwise chooses uniformly. The imitator's error was **24.98 ± 1.17%**. Temperature is the divisor in exponential score weighting that governs how widely moves are sampled. Among visit-trained V/Q rules, sampling from discounted Q VNM scores with fitted copies at a fixed temperature of 0.25 gave the lowest observed error, **25.43 ± 1.17%**, or **0.46 points** [0.11, 0.80] above the imitator. Choosing the temperature separately from observed move frequencies on each validation set gave **26.68 ± 1.37%**, or **1.70 points** [1.27, 2.14] above the imitator. The fixed-temperature ranking describes the tested alternatives after evaluation. Discounted V VNM with fitted copies again led the visit-trained rules in mean game points, **0.315 ± 0.075**, against **0.160 ± 0.038** for the randomized original. ## How the players were trained and what the scores could represent

Every source network saw nine cells and the side to move, passed these ten inputs through two layers of 128 units that keep positive inputs and zero negative inputs, and produced nine move scores. It minimized the negative natural logarithm of the probability assigned to the teacher's selected legal move using Adam, which scales updates using previous gradients, batches of 256 and initial step size 0.003. Training took **80–110 passes** over the 4,520 decision boards. Every network chose its assigned optimal move on **4,520/4,520** boards. Mean and largest guaranteed payoff given up were zero for each source. Against a different convention that chooses the lowest-numbered optimal cell, agreement ranged from **2,617 to 4,037 of 4,520**. [Frozen protocol and source settings](PHASE_IV_PROTOCOL.md) · [First source's certificate](experiments/phase_iv/block_00/source.json)

We kept rotations and reflections together. Across the 64 partitions, **729–1,111** decision boards were withheld. The two test sets for a source shared **117–317** boards. These are repetitions on one finite game. Independence for the statistical comparison comes from the 32 separately drawn source, partition, fit and opponent bundles. The six primary intervals attained half-widths of 0.91–1.85 percentage points for agreement and wins and 0.031–0.038 points per game. The design targets were approximately 3 percentage points and 0.06 points per game. [Planning simulation](experiments/phase_iv/planning.json) · [Actual partition and coverage counts](experiments/phase_iv/finite_bounds.jsonl.gz)

We started one complete game at every decision board for a fixed source and eight for its randomized version. Two source copies finished each game. Visits counted only when the parent belonged to the same partition as the game's start. All 4,520 boards were observed in every collection. A target is the observed count for a move divided by all observed visits to its parent. For the fixed sources this one-start design already identifies the chosen move at each board. Training only used the designated fitting rows. The V network also excluded resulting-board inputs shared with the reserved groups.

The V and Q networks used one randomly initialized layer of 64 or 192 units that retain positive activations and a fitted scalar output. V saw the resulting board, side to move, and counts of open lines and threats. Q also saw the current board description and indicators for the candidate cell. Inputs were standardized on the training examples. We minimized the sum of squared frequency errors plus a penalty on the squared output weights. Weights were solved directly. Validation error chose the width and a penalty of 0.01, 1 or 100. Each eligible legal candidate had equal weight. Only after fitting were predictions bounded to [0,1] for move selection. The separate move imitator used the source's two-layer architecture, trained all weights for 30 passes on the same observed move frequencies, and minimized the mean negative log probability of the observed action per board. The two fitting procedures have different capacities and losses. [Fitting code](src/policy_value_isomorph/phase_iv_fit.py) · [Imitation training](src/policy_value_isomorph/phase_iv_baseline.py)

**A value representation exists for these players.** Given the complete choice table, assign each resulting board a score strictly above the alternatives whenever the source chose it. Ordering the boards by those constraints produced **4,520/4,520** matches for every one of the 32 fixed sources using **7–9 distinct score levels**. These are constructed upper bounds on levels needed. The exact move lookup also copied every decision. Meanwhile **2,052 ± 38 out of 5,477** possible resulting boards were chosen from one parent but rejected from another. The minimum possible squared frequency error of a single number per resulting board was **0.08667 ± 0.00152** over all 16,167 legal board–move pairs. It is attained by averaging the target frequencies sharing each resulting board. Accurate ordering and exact frequency fitting are different requirements. For randomized players, even choosing their most probable move deterministically leaves **16.61 ± 0.26%** probability distance on withheld boards. The global optimum for a partially observed neural network remains open. ## Coverage, uncertain consequences and changed choices

- **Coverage.** With a budget of 2,048 observed fixed-source moves, playing only from the opening against itself visited exactly nine decision boards for each player. Mixed starts drawn from legal games covered **643 ± 22** boards. Starting preferentially at the least observed of 16 candidates covered **871 ± 20**. On withheld boards unreachable in ordinary self-play, direct Q agreement was **44.40 ± 4.58%**, **70.02 ± 3.39%**, and **70.40 ± 3.19%**, respectively. The fixed coverage comparison used 64 hidden units and an output-weight penalty of 1. [Opening protocols and paired coverage](experiments/phase_iv/diagnostics.jsonl.gz)
- **Restricted moves.** The planned sample of 32 multi-move boards per original gave **6,081/6,081** correctly ordered move pairs in 2,831 questions when we repeatedly asked for the favorite and removed it. We repeated the same exercise on the second partition, adding 32 board cases per original. Across both partitions the totals were **12,128/12,128** and 5,641 questions. At a budget of eight pairs per board, the actively balanced pair method recovered **10,792/12,128** and shuffled pairs **10,776/12,128**, using 16,384 questions each. Two further questions per board, shared by the three methods, compared a full-menu choice with its choice from a pair. When the player reversed its order whenever exactly two moves were offered, **10,080/12,128** pairs inferred through repeated removal agreed with the underlying unedited network ranking. The observed relation depended on the question posed. [Query histories, including unresolved pairs](experiments/phase_iv/block_00/menus.jsonl.gz) · [Uncertainty across all 32 sources](experiments/phase_iv/diagnostics.jsonl.gz)
- **Randomized moves.** For the ¾-priority mixture, direct counts from 1, 16 and 64 independent draws per withheld board gave probability distances **29.12 ± 0.64%**, **9.74 ± 0.26%** and **4.91 ± 0.12%**. With 64 draws, at least one possible move still had zero observed count on **59.5 ± 8.9** boards per partition averaged within players. Assigning zero probability to an event the player can produce makes the expected negative log probability infinite. Adding one artificial count per legal move removed those zeros but raised distance to **5.16 ± 0.14%**. The restricted version assigns ¾ to the favorite and ¼ to the runner-up, assigning all probability to the sole move on forced boards. Its results distinguish genuinely unavailable moves from possible moves missed by finite sampling. [Board-level counts](experiments/phase_iv/block_00/sampling.npz) · [Calibration by probability range](experiments/phase_iv/probability_calibration.jsonl.gz)
- **Fitting game payoffs.** When the randomized source finished both sides of a game, estimating the expected game points of a candidate from 1, 4 and 16 simulated endings yielded withheld squared errors of **0.2313 ± 0.0111**, **0.0578 ± 0.0025** and **0.0145 ± 0.0006** in squared game points. With fixed original players, a Q network trained directly on game points earned **−0.011 ± 0.077** points per game. Fitting the three outcome probabilities and then averaging game points earned **0.030 ± 0.078**. These are separate game-outcome training targets. The archives include exact supplied payoffs, original move versus equal-payoff or strictly worse alternatives, recovery by move-score gap, and optimal replies specific to each opponent. [Methods and error estimates](experiments/phase_iv/methods.jsonl.gz) · [Opponent-specific bounds](experiments/phase_iv/paired_games.jsonl.gz)
- **How move gaps and board rarity changed the result.** A move gap is the difference in exact expected points between the best move and the next best under the original continuation rule. For the fixed originals, Q trained on game points matched **33.44 ± 3.62%** of moves on withheld boards with a tie for first, and **84.37 ± 2.78%** where the gap exceeded one point. Forced moves are counted separately. For randomized originals, using 16 continuations instead of one reduced squared payoff error from **0.2625 ± 0.0144** to **0.0164 ± 0.0008** on common withheld boards, and from **0.1951 ± 0.0130** to **0.0122 ± 0.0007** on rare ones. Common boards have at least the median positive probability of being visited when the original plays itself. Rare boards have a lower positive probability. [Figure by score gap](experiments/phase_iv/return_gaps.png) · [Counts and paired uncertainty](experiments/phase_iv/return_groups.jsonl.gz)
- **Inferring a utility of winning, drawing and losing.** Holding a win at +1 and a loss at −1, we tried nine equally spaced values for a draw. Seven draw values tied for best agreement on the fixed sources' training choices on average. Choosing the tied value closest to zero yielded zero for all 64 partitions. With original players finishing both seats, that rule matched **71.09 ± 7.63%** of withheld moves. With fitted Q copies finishing both seats, the compact VNM rule matched **68.39 ± 7.01%** and earned **0.265 ± 0.083** points per game. This second comparison uses the same observed visits as Q direct and can play without the original. [Fits, equivalent utilities and access](experiments/phase_iv/block_00/soft.jsonl.gz) · [Independent and assisted comparisons](experiments/phase_iv/methods.jsonl.gz)
- **Changes in risk.** The board network has no input for an offered lottery, meaning a probability distribution over possible endings. We separately tested a player valuing a certain draw at 0.375 against win/loss gambles, and a player that always selects the first option displayed. On changed odds for the known-utility player, the fitted one-number VNM utility missed **6.68 ± 1.22%** of choices, an eight-band lookup learned from the same choices missed **6.36 ± 1.29%**, and the supplied draw value of zero missed **19.75 ± 2.57%**. The fitted grid selected 0.25 or 0.5, each 0.125 away from the known utility. Transferring that fitted utility to the presentation-driven player gave **50.37 ± 3.20%** error. Reading presentation order instead predicts that player's choices exactly. The two intervention families have different observable inputs, so their scores are reported for the players that actually accept those questions. [Recorded cases and predictions](experiments/phase_iv/block_00/intervention_cases.npz) · [Changed-choice rankings](experiments/phase_iv/rankings.json)

**Scope.** The constructed scores encode all these players' decisions. On withheld boards, how well those scores predict a choice depends on what was fitted, what the utility counts and who is assumed to finish the game. Earlier pilot comparisons used different original tie rules and fully trained hidden layers. This experiment varied source players, board partitions, opponent networks and reconstruction initializations together in the planned design. Attributing a difference from the pilot to any single design change would require a matched experiment. The complete finite game makes the lookup constructions possible. Predicting a player's response to a new kind of question still requires observing a player that can receive it. Checkpoint E is the next review.
