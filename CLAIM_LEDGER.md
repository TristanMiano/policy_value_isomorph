# Claims, evidence and open obligations

Each identifier stays attached to one question as its evidence changes. **PV-H** denotes a policy–value hypothesis family. Letters distinguish individual claims. A **policy** gives a move or distribution over available moves. A **decoder** turns numerical scores into behavior. A **contract** records the observations, rules, information at deployment and evaluated population. A **construction** exhibits an example, a **proof obligation** names a result to establish under explicit assumptions, and an **empirical prediction** specifies a magnitude to measure. Predictions below are research expectations, never acceptance criteria. [Research stance](RESEARCH_STANCE.md) · [Roadmap and task numbers](TODO.md)

## Existing evidence and its scope

- The original pilot fixed the population, both seats and the opponents before measuring. One reference player chooses the lowest-numbered move giving the best outcome against perfect replies. Another wins immediately, blocks a win, takes the center, takes a corner or takes any legal cell, using the lowest eligible number. Choosing the move giving the best game payoff when the source plays both sides thereafter matched the first player on 4,520/4,520 boards and the second on 3,432/4,520, a difference of 1,088 decisions (24.07 percentage points). These exact counts use one vote per reachable decision board. [Pilot checkpoint and definitions](TICTACTOE_PILOT.md)
- In the later visit-count study, a neural player chose the first reference player's move on all 4,520 boards. A score per resulting board inferred from the full choice table reproduced all 4,520 choices with seven score levels. The same resulting board had incompatible observed frequency labels across parents on 2,026/5,477 resulting boards. Reproducing the order needed for a choice and fitting its observed frequency are different obligations. [Constructions and measured limits](TICTACTOE_CHOICE_TRAINING.md)
- Checkpoint A fixed the public calls and the meaning of signed payoff. Reversing whose win is positive and reversing the selection direction changed 0/4,520 choices for the win/block/center/corner player. The pilot and Checkpoint A remain the records for those decisions. [Pilot decision](TODO.md#checkpoint-before-the-pytorch-migration) · [Checkpoint A](CHECKPOINT_A.md)

Checkpoint B fixes the proof obligations for representation, equivalence and utility, and the minimum game experiments for imitation, strength and predictions under changed conditions. Across 4,520 decision boards the priority player's 1,088 disagreements with exact game-payoff selection split into 853 equally good moves and 235 lower-return moves. A return-based explanation depends on the payoff, continuation and resolution of ties. A claim about the internal cause still requires interventions on the source. [Review and disposition of all seven claim families](CHECKPOINT_B.md)

The formal phase also supplies limits for claims that remain empirical. For PV-H2d, recovering a shared consequence utility must meet simultaneous choice constraints. For PV-H3a, a bound on score error predicts which moves could change but leaves actual fit-to-fit variation to measurement. Six ambiguity constructions identify what new evidence can distinguish explanations. [Expected utility](formalism/45_expected_utility.md) · [Stability](formalism/46_stability.md) · [Ambiguity and repairs](formalism/47_ambiguities.md)

## Claim register

Checkpoint D replayed how often fitted scores reproduce the original neural player's move. On 780 equally weighted withheld boards, using the fitted score of the final move of a complete play as utility and letting the original player finish both sides changed agreement by +7.91 percentage points for the network scoring the resulting board and −6.11 points for the network scoring the current board and move. Both differences are relative to the highest immediate score of the same fitted network. Dividing the sample standard deviation of the three paired differences by the square root of three gives a standard error of 0.52 and 0.94 points respectively. The same board split and opponent set were used throughout. [Rules, game outcomes and limits of the comparison](CHECKPOINT_D.md)

Checkpoint C fixes the exact two-way mapping for a declared set of observations. An attainable answer pattern identifies the group of all scores giving those answers. Selecting one example from the group defines a convention for encoding it. For the full tic-tac-toe choice table, three distinct board scores are necessary and enough with the source's lowest-cell rule for ties, compared with seven when every chosen move must have a unique highest score. Both copy 4,520/4,520 decisions. A payoff also depends on what the players who continue remember about earlier moves. [Checkpoint C review and remaining empirical questions](CHECKPOINT_C.md)

For the empirical entries, **agreement** is the fraction choosing the same move, higher meaning closer imitation. **Probability distance** is half the sum of absolute legal-move probability differences, averaged across boards, with zero meaning identical choices. **Gap** is the best score minus the next best in the mover's units. A **paired difference** subtracts two measurements on the same boards, seats or fits. Report variation over independent fits and the unit resampled. Knowable exact population counts carry no sampling error. Entries with an unknown effect distinguish a design reference or theoretical range from a measured magnitude.

**Self-review.** A perfect full-table fit can make the thesis appear stronger than evidence on new choices warrants. A poor fixed-payoff fit can make it appear weaker. H1a, H1e and H2d now have distinct data, comparisons and next obligations. One live alternative learns utility from common terminal consequences, another from time-weighted scores on complete paths. Changing the available continuations can distinguish them even when their opening choices coincide.

## Phase IV evidence from tic-tac-toe

Checkpoint E found that weighting the same withheld boards by their frequency in the original's own games can reverse the comparison. For the network scoring a board and candidate move, planning with a discounted sum of fitted frequencies changed agreement relative to taking its highest immediate score by +2.67 percentage points across equally weighted boards and −18.16 points across self-play visits. The paired 95% intervals across 32 originals were [2.23, 3.11] and [−24.80, −11.53] points. Retain the imitator, both weightings, the direct rule and the whole-game alternatives for the larger-game studies. The best tested predictor of a new move after removal was the imitator. The internal mechanism of the original player remains open. [Checkpoint E evidence and selected methods](CHECKPOINT_E.md) · [Spread, intervals and adjusted p-values](experiments/checkpoint_e.json)

- **Representing choices and fitting observed frequencies (H1, H2).** For each of 32 independently trained optimal players, one score per resulting board reproduced all 4,520 original choices. On average 2,052 of 5,477 resulting boards received conflicting numerical targets from different preceding boards, with a sample standard deviation of 38 across players. This establishes a construction for these players and measures a lower bound on frequency error. [Finite constructions](experiments/phase_iv/finite_bounds.jsonl.gz) · [Training error and disagreement examples](PHASE_IV.md#how-the-players-were-trained-and-what-the-scores-could-represent)
- **Choice and playing strength (H1e, H2d, H3).** Direct selection takes the highest immediate fitted move frequency. Von Neumann–Morgenstern (VNM) selection takes the move with the highest expected score of a completed game. With only the last move scored and both seats using fitted direct copies, VNM reduced withheld agreement by 5.39 percentage points for the resulting-board network and 11.29 for the board-and-move network. Its win-rate changes against the common panel were +4.19 and −3.04 points respectively. Counting earlier moves changed the result. - **Changed choices and randomized policies (H4, H5).** Relative rankings depend on whether the objective is copying, winning, or predicting the next choice after a move is removed. The experiments retain the consequences of missing rare choices, competing explanations for the same choices, and predictions on separately observed changed menus and lotteries. ## Phase V evidence across three games

A resulting-board score assigns one number to the board after a move. In 4×4 Reversi three score levels reproduce the prescribed optimal player's choice on all 56,621 reachable decision positions under the same row-order tie rule. Numerical move frequencies from preceding boards conflict at 4,124 of the 62,788 reachable resulting boards. In Connect Four one sampled original admits at most 3,658 of 3,659 choices with one score per resulting board under column-order ties. These are distinct constraints on exact representation and on learning a representation from visits (H1 and H2). [Constructions](experiments/phase_v/bounds.json) · [Scope](PHASE_V.md#how-close-can-the-representation-get)

For a network that scores the current board and candidate move, von Neumann–Morgenstern (VNM) selection compares the expected scores of complete games. When only the final move's signed fitted frequency counts and fitted direct copies continue, its average change against immediate selection was −28.46 ± 2.57 percentage points in move agreement and −8.05 ± 4.02 points in win rate (one-third weight each for tic-tac-toe, Connect Four and 4×4 Reversi, 32/24/24 independently trained originals). The paired 95% intervals were [−29.51, −27.41] and [−9.68, −6.41], adjusted p-values 4.70 × 10⁻³² and 8.46 × 10⁻¹² respectively. Changing the utility, continuation, opponent or decision population changes the comparison (H1e, H2d, H3). Predictions after removing moves measure behavioral preferences under that change (H4). ## Phase V follow-up on the two sources of disagreement

For each fixed-choice original, a board-and-move score equal to its observed frequency is 1 for its favorite and 0 for every alternative. In the perfect-fit control direct agreement was 100% throughout. After observing continuations following every candidate, selecting by the signed frequency of the final move gave 64.77 ± 5.56% training agreement in tic-tac-toe, 23.52 ± 9.34% in Connect Four and 87.65 ± 0.66% in 4×4 Reversi (32, 24 and 24 original blocks). Summing all signed frequencies with any discount strictly between zero and one preserves the fixed original's favorite when the full candidate paths have exact frequencies and both seats continue by fitted direct play. For arbitrary learned scores summing each played score minus the mover's best score, signed for its seat, preserves direct ordering under fitted greedy continuations. On withheld positions the controlled data and fitting interventions quantify separate sources of error.
