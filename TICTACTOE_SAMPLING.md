# How starting positions change recovery of a frozen tic-tac-toe player

A **decision board** is a legally reachable position where someone still has a move. The **source** is the frozen neural player whose choices we want to reproduce. **Agreement** is the fraction of boards on which a reconstruction chooses the source's exact move, including its preference between equally good moves. Tic-tac-toe has 4,520 decision boards. Starting from empty, two copies of this source visit exactly **nine**. Even when a uniformly random opponent can play every legal reply, the source can act on only **546** boards across both seats.

At a budget of 8,192 observed source moves, favoring rarely visited starting boards increased agreement on 780 held-out boards from **78.03% to 81.85%** for a network that scores the current board and candidate move directly. The gain was **3.82 ± 0.44 percentage points** over ordinary random starts. The ± quantity here and below is one **standard error**, the sample standard deviation of independent repetitions divided by the square root of their number. For differences we subtract measurements within each repetition before calculating it. We fitted five times and repeated data collection ten times. The uncertainty describes those repetitions with this fixed source and board split.

The earlier study already ran games from every board. For this deterministic source, which always chooses the same move on a given board, **one complete game per starting board produced the same normalized frequencies on all 16,167 legal board–move pairs as 32 games per board**. Across training, validation and test, this took 4,520 games and 10,313 source moves, down from 144,640 games and 330,016 moves. It retained 7,609 and 243,488 observations respectively after restricting each game's labels to its starting group. The one-start reconstruction matched 3,258 of 3,900 board–fit observations (83.54%) in the direct comparison, 1.69 ± 0.47 points above the rare-start procedure. Repeating the old deterministic games cannot resolve the remaining disagreement on withheld boards. [Original training and full-observation results](NEURAL_TICTACTOE_CONTROL.md)

For fitting alone, the 2,980 games started at training boards took **7,082** source moves and retained **5,702** training observations. That is fewer than the 8,192 collected by the random-start designs. The remaining 760 validation games and 780 test games took 1,596 and 1,635 moves, respectively.

## What was sampled

The source chooses the highest-scoring legal move from nine neural outputs. Its teacher considered every possible reply, chose the move giving the best guaranteed outcome, and broke ties by lowest cell number (0–8 in row order). This is the **minimax** rule. The source took the same move as its teacher on 4,520/4,520 decision boards, giving up zero payoff on the win/draw/loss scale of +1/0/−1. It learned only move labels. Its inputs were nine cells and the side to move, followed by two layers of 128 units that keep positive inputs and zero out negative ones. Training used all decision boards, batches of 256, the negative log probability of the target legal move, and Adam (an optimizer that scales updates using past gradients) at step size 0.003. The saved weights came from pass 80, initialization 7101. We loaded and checked these weights before sampling. [Source procedure and settings](NEURAL_TICTACTOE_CONTROL.md#which-neural-player-was-trained)

The fixed split keeps rotations and reflections together. It has 2,980 boards for fitting weights, 760 separate boards for choosing the training duration (validation), and 780 boards reserved for measuring agreement (test). A **source observation** is one action taken by the frozen player, including either copy in self-play. Random opponent actions and moves used to generate a starting board contribute zero labels. A count of zero visits to a board leaves every move frequency there undefined. At a visited board the training target for a move is its observed count divided by all source observations there.

| Collection rule | Start and continuation through game end |
| --- | --- |
| Empty, two copies (`empty_self`) | Start empty. Both sides play the source. |
| Empty, random opponent (`empty_random`) | Start empty. Alternate the source's seat each game. Its opponent chooses uniformly among legal moves. |
| Random starts (`random_openings`) | Start empty with probability ¼ and at a generated training board with probability ¾. Independently, half the games use two source copies and half use a uniformly random opponent. With a random opponent the source takes the first turn from a generated start, or a randomly chosen seat from empty. |
| Rarely visited starts (`low_visit_openings`) | Use the same start and opponent schedule. For each generated start, draw 16 candidates and use the one with the fewest source observations so far, resolving ties by draw order. Ordinary random starts take the first of the same 16 candidates. |

To generate a candidate, choose the number of moves already played uniformly from 0 through 8. Play random legal moves and retry at the same depth if the game ends. Draw 16,384 such candidates per repetition and retain training boards for restarts. This gives equal proposal probability to depths, with different probabilities for individual positions. The pool averaged 2,524.1 distinct training boards. Generating it took 101,213.7 random moves on average, including discarded attempts. The two mixed rules shared the pool, sampled with replacement and spent the same source-observation budget. Their environment and opponent move counts differ. We used the enumerated game tree to audit reach and cache the frozen player's one-board choices. Start selection itself needs only legal play, the board's split and observed counts.

Games ran to completion. We saved the first 512, 2,048 and 8,192 source observations. Additional turns needed to finish the game crossing a budget are recorded separately. Each budget includes source turns outside the training split, which are excluded from fitting. Fixed validation labels, source evaluation for scoring, and access to the source during whole-game move selection are additional resources outside that collection budget. ## Coverage and agreement

The first three numeric columns count distinct training boards observed out of 2,980, averaged over ten collections. The last two columns use the same five fitted networks at 8,192 observations. **Direct selection** takes the move with the highest immediate predicted frequency. **Von Neumann–Morgenstern (VNM) selection** evaluates each candidate by its expected learned utility through the end of the game, as defined below. Both columns use the network with current-board and candidate-move inputs.

| Collection rule | 512 observations | 2,048 observations | 8,192 observations | Direct agreement | VNM agreement |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Empty, two copies | 7.0 | 7.0 | 7.0 ± 0.0 | 46.28% | 52.97% |
| Empty, random opponent | 148.2 | 272.7 | 351.1 ± 0.8 | 56.13% | 71.87% |
| Random starts | 210.0 | 615.0 | 1,428.5 ± 7.1 | 78.03% | 80.62% |
| Rarely visited starts | 241.5 | 845.2 | 2,420.0 ± 7.9 | 81.85% | 83.54% |

For the direct rule these are 1,805, 2,189, 3,043 and 3,192 matches, respectively, out of five fits × 780 boards. Favoring rarely visited starts added **991.5 ± 7.3 training boards** relative to ordinary random starts. With an empty start and random opponent, only 365 training boards have any chance of a source turn. The observed 351.1 nonetheless account for 99.50% of the expected source turns on training boards against that opponent. Coverage by visits and coverage by distinct boards answer different questions.

**How common is a test board?** We summed the probability of every legal path from empty with a random opponent and equally likely source seats. Of the 780 test boards, 678 have zero probability of a source turn. Among the other 102, the median probability is 0.0021164 per game. The table compares direct selection on fixed populations across the same five fits. Its last column gives the paired change from ordinary to rarely visited starts in percentage points.

| Positive, at or below the median | 54 | 81.11% | 83.33% | +2.22 ± 1.59 |
| Above the median | 48 | 71.67% | 74.58% | +2.92 ± 2.43 |

The last two rows reweight the same test set. Under deterministic self-play only two test boards carry weight. A different question is agreement on *training* boards broken down by the number of observed source moves. With rarely visited starts, direct agreement averaged 81.74% on unvisited training boards (571.0 per fit), 93.03% on boards observed once (1,570.4), 92.03% on boards seen two to seven times (808.0), and 72.51% on boards seen eight or more times (30.6). Board difficulty and visit frequency vary together in these groups. Repeating a visit to this source supplies an identical move label. ## Move agreement and outcomes of complete games

A follow-up uses these same visits to compare ways of training the networks. It repeats direct and VNM selection on the original test positions and on a second partition. [Training changes and their effect on agreement](TICTACTOE_CHOICE_TRAINING.md)

Three networks predict visit frequency. **V** scores the resulting board alone (10 inputs). The **transition network** scores the current and resulting boards (20 inputs). **Q** scores the current board and candidate move (19 inputs). Each has two layers of 128 units with the source's activation and a single output converted to a number between zero and one by the logistic function. Each minimizes mean squared frequency error with Adam, batch size 256, initial step size 0.003, reduced to 0.00105 after pass 100. In each of 120 passes we shuffled observed candidates, cycling through them to hold training updates fixed at 4,980 examples for V or 10,864 for the other networks. For V we removed training inputs shared with validation or test, and validation inputs shared with test. All designs selected weights by the same validation error after the first and every tenth pass. For rarely visited starts the five fits averaged 4,087.6 distinct training candidates for V and 8,804.4 for each other network. The test set supplies no fitting or selection labels.

The **von Neumann–Morgenstern (VNM) expected-utility rule**, labeled “VNM discounted sum” in the table, takes a candidate move, then uses the frozen source for both sides to the end of standard tic-tac-toe. The utility of that complete continuation is the sum of its predicted move frequencies, positive for X moves and negative for O moves. Multiply each term by 0.9 raised to the number of moves since the candidate, whose weight is one. X maximizes and O minimizes the expected sum. One continuation per candidate gives its exact expectation for this deterministic source. Both decoders break ties by lowest cell number. The direct decoder needs only its fitted network at move selection. The VNM rule additionally queries the frozen source during planning.

For game strength we summed all outcome probabilities from the empty board. X and O each have half weight. Four opponent families each have one-quarter weight. They play uniform legal moves, use five untrained networks (each takes its highest-scoring legal move and shares its family's weight equally), use a priority rule (first applicable of immediate win, block, center, corner, any legal move), or play minimax. The latter three resolve ties by lowest cell number. The table gives five-fit means ± one standard error under the rarely visited start rule. Outcome probabilities are exact for each fitted player and opponent. The source row is fixed.

| Player and selection | Agreement on 780 test boards | Win | Draw | Loss |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Frozen source | 100% | 42.51% | 57.49% | 0.00% |
| V, direct | 68.10 ± 0.53% | 38.76 ± 0.94% | 30.16 ± 6.50% | 31.08 ± 5.91% |
| V, VNM discounted sum | 75.08 ± 0.65% | 42.18 ± 0.99% | 55.14 ± 2.86% | 2.68 ± 2.53% |
| Transition, direct | 78.49 ± 0.64% | 40.27 ± 0.70% | 42.88 ± 0.64% | 16.85 ± 0.83% |
| Transition, VNM discounted sum | 82.82 ± 0.84% | 47.48 ± 4.27% | 43.23 ± 3.87% | 9.29 ± 3.63% |
| Q, direct | 81.85 ± 0.33% | 41.54 ± 0.06% | 27.04 ± 5.96% | 31.43 ± 5.90% |
| Q, VNM discounted sum | 83.54 ± 0.79% | 46.14 ± 3.43% | 49.19 ± 3.20% | 4.67 ± 2.99% |

With ordinary random starts, direct Q won 41.17%, drew 29.91% and lost 28.93%. Favoring rarely visited starts increased direct Q agreement by 3.82 points while its loss rate rose 2.50 ± 11.59 points. With whole-game selection, the same collection change raised Q agreement by 2.92 ± 0.91 points and lowered mean losses from 23.80% to 4.67%, a decrease of 19.13 ± 6.80 points. All rates describe this opponent mix. [Every design, opponent and seat](experiments/tictactoe_sampling/games.csv)

## How many times to revisit a board

For a **stochastic source**, a player that draws its move from a probability distribution, we also tested the randomized priority rule. Independently on each turn it follows the priority player above with probability ¾ and chooses a uniformly random legal move otherwise. Its exact probabilities are known for auditing counts. **Total variation distance** is half the sum of absolute differences between observed and true move probabilities on a board. Zero means identical probabilities and one means disjoint choices.

At 8,192 source observations, ordinary random starts reached 1,649.4 ± 6.4 training boards and had mean distance 0.2454 ± 0.0016 **on those observed boards**. Favoring rarely visited starts reached 2,544.8 ± 8.7 and had distance 0.2672 ± 0.0011 on its broader observed set. The common population comparison comes from starting at every board. On the same 2,980 training boards, one start per board gave mean distance **0.2754** and left **7,454** possible board–move pairs unobserved. With 32 starts the distance was **0.0598** and **849** possible pairs remained unobserved. These two figures per design describe one fixed collection each. This check measures the training labels. The five-fit network comparison used the deterministic neural source.

We also compared the two random-start rules on identical boards at 8,192 observations. On the 1,568.5 ± 7.4 training boards observed by both rules in a repetition, ordinary starts had mean distance 0.2434 ± 0.0017 and rarely visited starts had 0.2531 ± 0.0017. To score all 2,980 boards, we used empirical frequencies wherever observed and assigned equal probability to each legal move only at unseen boards. With this stated fallback the distances were **0.3677** and **0.3054**, a reduction of **0.0624 ± 0.0013** across ten paired collections. This calculation uses the raw move counts. Training still excludes boards with no source observations. [Probability errors on matched populations](experiments/tictactoe_sampling/randomized_frequencies.csv)

For this deterministic tic-tac-toe source I would collect one game from every legal decision board when the full list is available. For a larger game, the tested alternative is to reserve some games for the natural opening, generate legal starts at several depths, favor boards with few source observations, and record whose moves entered the counts. For a randomized player, budget independent repeat moves at previously observed boards as well. Evaluate with separate, fixed sets representing both ordinary play and unusual positions, publishing results for each and specifying the weights of any average. This experiment quantifies the tradeoff for one game. It gives no estimate of the needed budget for a larger board.

## Reproduction and field definitions

Install the optional dependencies with `python -m pip install -e ".[neural-control]"`. From the repository root run `python scripts/run_tictactoe_sampling.py` to reproduce and compare the numerical files, or pass `--output-dir` with a new directory. Collection seeds are 9101–9110, with 1000 added for the independent opening pools. Seeds 7201–7205 initialize the five fits paired across designs. The run uses a single CPU thread. [Executable experiment](scripts/run_tictactoe_sampling.py) · [Sampling functions](src/policy_value_isomorph/coverage_sampling.py)

In the agreement and game tables, `selection` identifies the original source, direct selection or VNM discounted-sum selection.

The collection and trained-model replay reproduced the archived move counts, agreements, game probabilities and fitted weights byte for byte. An independent calculation from the move counts reproduced the matched probability errors. Repository verification with `pytest -q` passed 69 tests. `python scripts/demo_tictactoe.py` printed sample boards, recovered values and moves.
